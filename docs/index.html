Samanvaya

Building Grammar-Aware Resources and Annotation Standards for Indian Languages

A unified, grammar-aware framework and tagging system for creating and annotating multilingual corpora across Indian languages — preserving the morphology, honorifics, evidentiality, and kāraka relations that whitespace tokenization and generic annotation schemes routinely lose.

Motivation

Why Indian languages need a grammar-aware layer

Progress in multilingual large language models, machine translation, and cross-lingual NLP has created a pressing need for high-quality Indian language resources. Initiatives such as Universal Dependencies, the IIIT‑Hyderabad/LTRC annotation framework, and language-specific corpora have made considerable progress — yet several characteristic features of Indian languages remain difficult to capture uniformly, and their underlying unity is often overlooked.

Indian languages exhibit rich morphology, agglutinative structure, productive participial formations, complex verbal systems, evidential and reportative markers, honorific distinctions, relative‑correlative constructions, and grammar-driven semantic relations described in detail within the Indian grammatical tradition. These phenomena are frequently simplified or normalized during corpus creation, tokenization, annotation, and translation — leading to information loss and reduced linguistic fidelity.

The Samanvaya initiative addresses these concerns through a unified grammar-aware framework for creating and annotating multilingual corpora in Indian languages. It adds a layer that preserves language-specific grammatical information for computational use. This tutorial explains the Samanvaya tagging system for grammar-aware resource creation and its relevance to future Indian language NLP systems.

Relevance

Why this tutorial fits ICON 2026

ICON has long been the leading forum for NLP research on Indian languages. As the field moves toward multilingual foundation models, agentic systems, and LLM-driven NLP, consistent, grammar-aware linguistic resources are increasingly important.

This tutorial addresses questions central to that shift: how parallel corpora can preserve grammatical features across Indian languages; whether tokenization can be standardized across agglutinative and non-agglutinative languages; how annotation frameworks can better capture phenomena unique to Indian languages; what role traditional Indian grammatical knowledge can play in enhancing AI models; and how linguistically informed resources can advance machine translation, parsing, and multilingual LLMs. Its goal is to foster collaboration among linguists, NLP researchers, corpus developers, and AI practitioners toward shared community standards for grammar-aware Indian language resources.

Tutorial Overview

Five sessions, one interlingual framework

Participants are introduced to Samanvaya's conceptual foundations, tokenization strategies for morphologically rich languages, lexical, morphological, and syntactic annotation schemes, and its emerging interplay with large language models. The tagset currently covers Sanskrit, Kannada, Bangla, Marathi, Malayalam, and Hindi, with conventions to extend to other Indian languages.

01
45 min

Motivation for the Samanvaya Framework: A Unified Interlingua for Indian Languages

The need for a common interlingual representation for multilingual Indian‑language NLP; Samanvaya's complementarity with Universal Dependencies and the LTRC tagset; limitations of existing schemes in representing language‑specific phenomena; an overview of applications in multilingual NLP and AI.

02
30 min

Tokenization and Word Grouping for Indian Languages

Why whitespace-based tokenization misses relevant grammatical units, and how grammar-aware word grouping addresses this — canonical tokenization, compound predicates, auxiliary and modal verb chains, correspondence between agglutinative and non-agglutinative languages, and cross-lingual token alignment.

03
30 min

Lexical and Morphological Annotation using Samanvaya

Token types and stem representation, and native grammar-aware morphological categories — TAM (tense, aspect, mood), PNG (person, number, gender), case marking, respect and honorification, inclusivity, negation, evidentiality, emphasis, classifiers, and clitic-fused dependency information in agglutinative languages.

04
30 min

Dependency and Sentence-Level Annotation using Samanvaya

Syntactic annotation beyond conventional dependency parsing — kāraka and non-kāraka relations, coordination, relative‑correlative and comparative constructions, conditional and causal clauses, quotative structures, copular and verbless sentences, and finite/non-finite constructions.

05
30 min

Samanvaya and Large Language Models

The two-way relationship between Samanvaya and LLMs — few-shot prompting for candidate tags, Samanvaya-tagged corpora as structured supervision for tokenization and kāraka-consistent argument structure, evaluating LLM-generated annotations against gold-standard data, and open questions in grammatically faithful generation.

Running time: 2 hours 45 minutes (2.5–3 hour slot).

Coverage

The Samanvaya tagset is developed and validated across Sanskrit, Hindi, Marathi, Bangla, Kannada, and Malayalam — spanning four scripts — with conventions designed to extend to further Indian languages.
